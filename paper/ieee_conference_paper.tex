\begin{abstract}
Fine-grained image classification remains difficult because closely related categories often differ through subtle shape, texture, color, and part-level details. Synthetic augmentation offers a possible way to improve data diversity, but indiscriminately adding generated images can also introduce noise, class drift, and visually implausible samples. This paper presents a practical AGA-inspired selective synthetic augmentation framework for fine-grained bird classification on CUB-200-2011. The framework first generates synthetic bird-image candidates and then admits only selected samples into final training using classifier confidence, CLIP semantic alignment, DINO-based diversity checking, class-balance control, and optional attribute-aware coverage. In the saved repository run, the real-only baseline achieved 0.1388 test accuracy and 0.1111 macro F1, while adding all synthetic candidates reduced performance to 0.1367 accuracy and 0.1025 macro F1. In contrast, selective admission improved performance to 0.1505 accuracy and 0.1206 macro F1. The ablation results show that confidence-based screening was the strongest individual signal, while the broader pipeline remained useful for building a conservative and interpretable selection process. The work is presented as a reproducible empirical study under constrained compute, not as a direct superiority claim over the original AGA paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Fine-grained visual recognition is challenging because different categories often share the same global object structure and vary only through subtle local characteristics. In bird classification, these characteristics may include beak shape, crown color, breast pattern, wing markings, tail structure, and small texture differences. The CUB-200-2011 benchmark is therefore a standard and meaningful testbed for studying fine-grained classification difficulty \cite{wah2011}.

Synthetic augmentation is appealing in this setting because it may expand the visual diversity of training data without requiring additional manual collection. However, generated data is not automatically helpful. In fine-grained tasks, even small distortions can damage class identity. If synthetic images contain incorrect species cues, unrealistic anatomy, or noisy background-context interactions, they may weaken rather than strengthen the training signal.

This paper studies a practical alternative: selective admission of synthetic samples. Rather than treating all generated images as equally useful, the proposed framework evaluates each candidate and accepts only a small subset judged to be sufficiently reliable for final training. The approach is described as AGA-inspired because it follows the motivation of automated generative augmentation while using a practical local pipeline suited to the available data, tools, and compute budget.

The main contribution of this work is not a state-of-the-art claim. Instead, the paper offers a reproducible, evidence-driven workflow showing that synthetic sample quality can matter more than synthetic sample quantity. In the saved experiments, a carefully selected synthetic subset improved over both a real-only baseline and a naive all-synthetic control.

The specific contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a practical AGA-inspired selective synthetic augmentation framework for fine-grained bird classification;
    \item a reproducible experimental workflow with saved manifests, logs, tables, plots, confusion matrices, and qualitative sample grids;
    \item an internal empirical comparison showing that selected synthetic samples improved performance while unfiltered synthetic expansion did not;
    \item a claim-safe interpretation that clearly separates internal experimental gains from unsupported direct superiority claims over the base AGA paper.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Fine-Grained Bird Classification}
Fine-grained recognition has long been studied because it requires models to identify subtle inter-class differences that are often difficult even for human observers. The CUB-200-2011 dataset provides 200 bird species with image labels, bounding boxes, and attributes, making it one of the most widely used benchmarks for this problem \cite{wah2011}. Practical deep learning baselines for this domain commonly use convolutional backbones such as ResNet because they offer a strong and reproducible starting point \cite{he2016}.

\subsection{Synthetic Augmentation}
Classical augmentation improves generalization through operations such as cropping, flipping, color jittering, and geometric perturbation. Generative augmentation aims to go further by creating new training samples with broader appearance variation. However, the usefulness of generative augmentation depends on whether the generated images preserve task-relevant class identity. This issue becomes especially important in fine-grained recognition, where small artifacts may significantly alter the meaning of a sample \cite{rahat2025}.

\subsection{Semantic and Diversity-Based Filtering}
CLIP provides transferable image-text representations that can be used to estimate whether an image is semantically aligned with its intended class description \cite{radford2021}. DINO-style self-supervised features provide strong visual embeddings for measuring similarity and detecting near-duplicate samples \cite{caron2021}. These signals are attractive for synthetic data filtering because they support semantic agreement checks, redundancy control, and more conservative admission of generated samples.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Dataset and Split}
All experiments use the CUB-200-2011 bird dataset \cite{wah2011}. The prepared repository split contains 5,394 training images, 600 validation images, and 5,794 test images across 200 classes. Optional image-level attribute metadata is preserved for the attribute-aware variant of the selection pipeline.

Fig.~\ref{fig:real_distribution} shows the training-set distribution used in the saved experiments. The distribution is nearly balanced, which is important because it reduces the risk that the main result is simply an artifact of heavy real-data imbalance.

\subsection{Synthetic Candidate Generation}
The candidate-generation stage is implemented as a practical approximation of the original AGA idea rather than an exact reproduction of the published method. The repository preserves bird foreground structure using dataset information and foreground estimation, then introduces controlled appearance and context variation to create synthetic candidates. This design keeps the workflow reproducible while still producing a meaningful candidate pool for downstream filtering.

Representative synthetic candidates are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_candidates}. \subsection{Selective Sample Admission}
Each synthetic candidate is evaluated before it is allowed into the final training set. The selection process uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item classifier confidence for class-consistency screening;
    \item CLIP image-text alignment for semantic agreement with the intended class;
    \item DINO feature similarity for diversity control and near-duplicate detection;
    \item class-balance control to avoid synthetic overrepresentation;
    \item optional attribute-aware coverage to preserve fine-grained visual cues.
\end{itemize}

The admission strategy is intentionally conservative. In the saved run, 400 synthetic candidates were generated and only 15 were accepted, corresponding to a 3.75\% admission rate. Fig.~\ref{fig:selection_funnel} visualizes this process and reinforces the central idea of the paper: the method prioritizes reliability over synthetic volume.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_selection_funnel.png}
\caption{Selective admission funnel. Out of 400 generated candidates, only 15 were admitted for final training, giving a 3.75\% acceptance rate.}
\label{fig:selection_funnel}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Training Protocol}
The repository preserves three main experimental settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{exp1\_real\_only}: supervised training on real data only;
    \item \texttt{exp2\_real\_plus\_all\_synthetic}: training on real data plus all generated synthetic candidates;
    \item \texttt{exp3\_real\_plus\_selected\_synthetic}: training on real data plus only the final selected synthetic subset.
\end{itemize}

The saved selected-synthetic run uses a ResNet18 backbone with 224-pixel inputs. The manuscript reports the preserved single-run outputs directly and does not claim a completed multi-seed benchmark study.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Main Experimental Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the saved main experiment outputs. The selected-synthetic setting achieved the best test accuracy and the best macro F1 among the three internal comparisons.

\midrule

The visual summary in Fig.~\ref{fig:main_internal_comparison} makes the pattern clear. Compared with the real-only baseline, selective admission improved test accuracy by 0.0117 absolute points, which corresponds to an 8.4\% relative gain. Compared with the naive all-synthetic condition, selective admission improved test accuracy by 0.0138 absolute points, or approximately 10.1\% relative improvement. Macro F1 follows the same pattern, increasing from 0.1111 to 0.1206 over the real-only baseline and from 0.1025 to 0.1206 over the naive synthetic setting.

This is the core empirical result of the paper: simply adding more synthetic data did not help, but selecting a small and more reliable subset did.

\section{Discussion}
The strongest message from the saved results is straightforward: synthetic augmentation was beneficial only when it was selective. The all-synthetic condition slightly underperformed the real-only baseline, which indicates that synthetic quantity by itself was not useful in this setup. By contrast, the selected-synthetic setting improved both accuracy and macro F1, showing that a small curated subset can be more valuable than a larger unfiltered synthetic pool.

The ablation results refine this story. Confidence-based screening appears to be the strongest single mechanism in the saved run, but the broader pipeline still has methodological value because it provides semantic checking, diversity control, class-balance reasoning, and an interpretable structure for deciding which samples should be trusted. In that sense, the full framework is useful even when every component does not produce a separate gain in one particular run.

The loss-curve evidence also adds an important interpretation. Training improved smoothly across epochs, while validation loss decreased more slowly and began to flatten. This suggests that the selected-synthetic experiment learned meaningful structure without catastrophic instability, but it also indicates that the current setup remains limited in generalization. A stronger backbone, more extensive hyperparameter tuning, or repeated multi-seed experiments may reveal larger and more consistent gains.

\section{Limitations}
This work has several important limitations. First, the generation stage is AGA-inspired rather than an exact reproduction of the published method. Second, the strongest evidence comes from saved single-run outputs rather than a full multi-seed benchmark with means and standard deviations. Third, compute constraints limited the scale of repeated experimentation and model exploration. Fourth, the absolute accuracy remains low for a 200-class fine-grained benchmark, so the result should be treated as a controlled empirical finding rather than a competitive leaderboard claim. Fifth, the repository does not currently support a fair like-for-like improvement claim over the base AGA paper.

These limitations narrow the strongest defensible claims, but they do not remove the value of the contribution. The framework is complete, reproducible, and supported by preserved quantitative and qualitative evidence.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented a practical AGA-inspired selective synthetic augmentation framework for fine-grained bird classification on CUB-200-2011. In the saved repository run, selective synthetic admission improved test accuracy from 0.1388 to 0.1505 and macro F1 from 0.1111 to 0.1206 relative to the real-only baseline, while the naive all-synthetic setting slightly reduced performance. The qualitative samples, selection statistics, ablation evidence, confusion matrix, and loss curves collectively support a focused conclusion: in the tested setup, carefully selected synthetic samples were more useful than blind synthetic expansion. As a reproducible empirical study with explicit internal controls and preserved artifacts, the work provides a credible foundation for future higher-budget and multi-seed studies of synthetic data selection in fine-grained recognition.
